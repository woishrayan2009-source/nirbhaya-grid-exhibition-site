The alert doesn't wait for a hand that's free.

A second, automatic path into the same alert chain as a manual panic button — so a fall in heart rate signal never depends on someone being conscious, unrestrained, and able to press anything.

Context

What Already Exists

Manual panic-button wearables — a pendant, a ring, a phone app with a hold-to-trigger button — are already a well-established category, sold widely and built into several existing personal-safety products. They work: they send an SMS or call with a live location the moment someone presses them. The category isn't new, and we're not presenting it as if it were.

Their shared weak point is the precondition baked into all of them: the wearer has to be conscious, free to move a hand, and able to physically find and press a button. That's precisely the condition that often isn't met in a real assault.

Our Contribution

What We Changed

We add a low-cost heart-rate and accelerometer anomaly detector as an automatic secondary trigger, running alongside — never replacing — the manual button. A sudden, sharp spike in heart rate combined with irregular, forceful motion is enough to fire the same alert chain on its own.

That alert chain is the one already proven in Extingo and Rakshak — SMS, a call, and a live location — so this module doesn't invent a new gadget or a new app; it plugs a missing trigger path into infrastructure that already works.
